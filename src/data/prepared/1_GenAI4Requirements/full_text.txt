NoRBERT: Transfer Learning for Requirements Classification
Summary: The paper introduces NoRBERT, a BERT fine tuning approach for automatically
classifying natural language software requirements, aimed at fixing the common issue that
traditional feature based ML models lose accuracy on unseen projects due to changes in
wording and style. Using the PROMISE NFR dataset and a relabeled version, the authors
evaluate several tasks including functional vs non functional classification, predicting major
non functional subclasses, and distinguishing functional versus quality aspects, showing
NoRBERT matches or outperforms prior work while requiring no manual feature engineering
or preprocessing and maintaining strong performance in cross project evaluations. They also
label the functional aspect requirements with concern tags Function, Data, and Behavior and
release this dataset, then show NoRBERT can detect Function and Behavior well but
struggles more on Data mainly because the class is underrepresented and recall is low.
Three positive point:
1. Strong generalization to unseen projects: Authors don’t just report random
cross-validation. They explicitly test project-aware splits and show NoRBERT stays
accurate even when the test requirements come from projects the model never saw
before.
2. Minimal manual work and clean pipeline: NoRBERT removes the need for
handcrafted linguistic features and heavy text preprocessing used in older
approaches, which makes the method simpler to reproduce and easier to apply to
new datasets.
3. New useful labeled dataset and extra task: Beyond benchmarking on PROMISE
and the relabeled dataset, they add and release a new annotation layer for
functional requirements (Function, Data, Behavior) and demonstrate it can be
learned reasonably well, opening the door to more fine grained requirements
analysis.
Three Negative points:
1. Small and imbalanced datasets limit what the results prove
PROMISE is relatively small and many NFR subclasses have very few examples, so
performance can look strong on common classes but drop on rare ones, and the
model has limited chance to learn minority categories well.
2. Lower performance on harder or underrepresented labels (especially “Data”)
In their new Function Data Behavior labeling, the model is noticeably weaker on the
Data concern, mainly because Data examples are fewer and the classifier misses
many true Data cases (low recall).
3. Less interpretability
It is harder to explain exactly why a requirement was assigned a label, which can

matter in requirements engineering practice.
EXAM:
What is the context of this work?
This paper is in the area of requirements engineering and natural language processing.
It studies how to automatically classify software requirements written in plain
language, especially into functional and non functional types. Earlier methods often
worked on known datasets, but their performance became worse on unseen projects
because wording and writing style change from project to project. The authors use
BERT, a transfer learning language model, because it can learn general language
patterns from large text data and then be adapted to a smaller task. Their system is
called NoRBERT, and they test it on the PROMISE NFR dataset, a relabeled version of
that dataset, and a new functional concern dataset they created.
What problem is this work trying to address?
The main problem is that existing requirement classification methods do not generalize
well to new and unseen projects. Many older approaches depend too much on project
specific wording, sentence style, or manual preprocessing, so they are less useful
in real practice. The paper tries to build a classifier that keeps good performance on
both seen and unseen projects, while also improving classification of non functional
subclasses and functional concerns.
Methodology
What is the study design?
The authors fine-tune pre-trained BERT models, both base and large versions, to classify
requirements, and they call the approach NoRBERT. They test it on four tasks: functional
vs non functional classification, non functional subclass classification, classification
on the relabeled functional and quality aspect dataset, and a new task for classifying
functional requirements into function, data, and behavior. They use established
datasets, especially the PROMISE NFR dataset and the relabeled version by Dalpiaz et al.,
and they also manually label functional requirements to create a new dataset. To evaluate
performance, they use precision, recall, and F1 score, with settings such as 10 fold
cross validation, project specific folds, and leave one project out (10 fold cross
validation splits all data into 10 parts and tests each part once, project specific folds train
on some projects and test on different projects, and leave one project out trains on all
projects except one and tests on that left out project.) They also compare NoRBERT directly
with earlier published methods to see whether transfer learning improves results.

Why have the authors chosen this particular methodology?
They chose transfer learning (Transfer learning is a machine learning technique
where a model developed for one task is reused as the starting point for a second,
related task) with BERT because requirements engineering usually has only a small
amount of labeled data, and BERT can work well even with limited task-specific data.
They also wanted a method that needs less manual feature engineering and preprocessing
than older approaches. Most importantly, they wanted to test whether a transfer learning
model can generalize better to unseen projects, which is the main weakness of previous
work.
What is the methodology attempting to evaluate?
The methodology evaluates whether NoRBERT improves requirement classification
performance compared with earlier methods. It also evaluates whether the model can still
perform well when tested on new projects it did not see during training. In addition, it
checks whether transfer learning can classify not only broad categories, but also fine grained
subclasses such as non functional subclasses and functional concerns like function, data,
and behavior.
Results
What is the key takeaway/lessons learned of this paper? (3 sentences)
The main takeaway is that NoRBERT works very well and usually matches or beats
previous methods, especially on harder settings with unseen projects. It performs
similarly on basic functional vs non functional classification, but it is stronger on non
functional subclasses, the relabeled dataset, and the new functional concern task, while
avoiding manual preprocessing. The biggest lesson is that transfer learning makes
requirements classification more practical, because it improves generalization and can
work with limited labeled data.
Automated Handling of Anaphoric Ambiguity in Requirements: A
Multi-solution Study
This paper studies how to automatically handle pronoun ambiguity in natural language
requirements by comparing six different NLP and machine learning approaches on about
1,350 industrial requirements. It finds that supervised machine learning is best for
detecting ambiguous pronouns (about 60% precision with 100% recall). At the same

time, a fine tuned SpanBERT model is best for resolving pronouns to the right
antecedent (about 98% success). Hence, the authors recommend a hybrid workflow that
uses ML to flag risky cases and SpanBERT to suggest likely references when ambiguity is
low.
Three positive points:
1. Comprehensive comparison that gives actionable guidance: The paper does a
true multi solution study with six alternative approaches (language model, supervised
ML, and off the shelf coreference tools), so it can be learn which technology fits
which task instead of seeing only one proposed method.
1. Evaluation on realistic industrial requirements with careful annotation: It uses a
large and diverse industrial dataset (DAMIR: 22 specifications across 8 domains,
1,251 requirements, 737 pronoun occurrences) labeled by two independent non
author annotators, and reports moderate agreement (κ = 0.54), which supports
credibility and generalizability.
2. Clear performance win and practical recommendation: The results are strong
and easy to apply: best ambiguity detection achieves ≈60% precision and 100%
recall, and best resolution achieves ≈98% success rate, leading to a simple
recommended hybrid workflow (ML for detection, SpanBERT for resolution).
Three negative points:
1. Annotation agreement is only moderate, and ambiguity labels partly come from
disagreement: they report Fleiss’ κ = 0.54, which they call “moderate agreement,”
and when annotators disagreed for a pronoun, the authors treat that pronoun as
ambiguous and label all its triples inconclusive. This is reasonable, but it also means
the ground truth for “ambiguous” is closely tied to annotator disagreement rather than
an independent gold standard definition.
2. Precision for ambiguity detection is still around 60%, so it can create many
false alarms: the best ambiguity detector (MLensemble) achieves 100% recall but
only ~60% precision, which implies a substantial number of false positives that
analysts would need to filter manually.
3. Generalizability is not fully settled and needs more validation in practice Even
though DAMIR spans eight domains, the authors explicitly note external validity
threats and say that more documents and user studies are needed to more
conclusively evaluate usefulness.
EXAM
What is the context of this work?
This paper is about requirements engineering and natural language processing. It

studies anaphoric ambiguity, which happens when a pronoun like it or they can
refer to more than one possible thing in a requirement. This kind of ambiguity is
common in natural language requirements and can cause different readers to
understand the same requirement differently. The authors focus on automating both
ambiguity detection and anaphora resolution, meaning finding whether a pronoun
is ambiguous and, when possible, identifying what it refers to. They compare several
modern and traditional AI and NLP methods on industrial requirements to see which
works best.
What problem is this work trying to address?
The paper tries to solve the problem that pronouns in requirements can be unclear and
misleading, which may cause misunderstandings in software projects. Previous work
in requirements engineering used older NLP and ML methods and was often tested
on small datasets or only one domain. The authors want a more accurate and
practical automated solution for both detecting ambiguous pronouns and
resolving clear anaphora in real industrial requirements.
What is the study design?
The study is a comparative experimental study where the authors build and compare
six different automated solutions for the same problem, which is handling pronoun
ambiguity in requirements. These six solutions are grouped into three families: two
SpanBERT based solutions (LLM), three supervised machine learning
solutions, and one NLP coreference solution built from existing tools. Before
testing, they preprocess each requirement to detect pronouns, collect the nearby
text as context, and generate all possible noun phrases that the pronoun might refer
to. Then they train, fine tune, and tune the models using datasets including
CoNLL2011, ReqEval, and their own industrial dataset DAMIR, which contains
requirements from several domains. Finally, they compare all solutions on three
things: how well they detect ambiguity, how well they resolve the pronoun, and how
much time they take to run.
Why have the authors chosen this particular methodology?
They chose this methodology because there are many possible NLP and ML
technologies, and they wanted to compare them side by side instead of assuming
one is best. They also wanted to check whether recent language models like BERT
and SpanBERT are actually better than traditional methods for this requirements
task. A multi solution experiment helps them find not only the best tool, but also
useful lessons for other requirements engineering automation problems.
What is the methodology attempting to evaluate?

The methodology evaluates which solution is best at detecting anaphoric ambiguity in
requirements. It also evaluates which solution is best at resolving pronouns,
meaning identifying the correct antecedent when possible. In addition, it checks the
execution time of each solution to see whether the methods are practical to use.
What is the key takeaway/lessons learned of this paper?
The main takeaway is that different tasks need different best solutions. For
ambiguity detection, the best result comes from supervised machine learning,
especially the ensemble model, with about 60 percent precision and 100 percent
recall. For anaphora resolution, the best result comes from SpanBERTRE (LLM),
which reaches about 98 percent success rate, so the authors recommend a hybrid
approach that combines ML for detection and SpanBERT for resolution.
