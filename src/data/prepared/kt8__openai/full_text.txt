History
In 1988, Steve Dorner wrote an eMail client for the Macintosh that soon
grew to have an army of very satisfied users. It was named "Eudora" in
honour of American authoress Eudora Welty, who wrote a short story
called "Why I Live at the P.O." Eudora quickly gained a legion of fans and
was bought out by Qualcomm in 1991. Soon after that, it was re-written
("ported", in computerese) for PC's as well. Fast forward to 2006 and,
amidst shifting corporate priorities, Qualcomm Eudora was killed off, to be
replaced by a pale imitation called Eudora OSE. This was not well received
and soon died as well.
Eudora still has its fans, though, which is why we're bringing it back! This
year, its code was open-sourced and given to the Computer History
Museum. Seeing that nobody had taken on the monumental task of
reviving this excellent piece of software and updating it to comply with
today's standards, I picked up development myself, along with a talented
team of C++ programmers.
Legal encumbrances prevented us from using the Eudora name, so the
program was re-christened Hermes, after the Greek personification of
business and patron deity of messengers. We plan to release the
Windows version in Q1 2019.

We are debating two alternatives; the choice between them is dependent
on the fact that Eudora was actually two programs, written in different
languages (C and C++) on separate platforms (x86 and Power PC, in those
days) by parallel teams of developers. One idea is to abandon Macintosh
Eudora and to write a Macintosh interface from scratch, with the code as a
whole coming from the Windows version. The benefits of this approach
are that it's economical in respect to time (given that the Windows code is
more complete and up-to-date) and cost (given that only one development
team need exist). The drawback is that people who have grown up on
Macintosh Eudora might be unused to the Windows version running on
their computer.
The other idea is to open a separate project, with the code coming from
the Macintosh version. This has the benefit of being more specific to OS X
as well as UNIX, but besides duplicating time, energy, and cost, the
Macintosh code is less complete, obsolescent (it's written in Carbon rather
than Cocoa), and uses obsolete tools (like the CodeWarrior integrated
development environment).
As work proceeds, we will know better which is the right approach to
follow. We are currently pursuing both; it may be that we won't know until
shortly before launch which version will receive official support, and which
will receive community support. In the world of software development,
such an occurrence is not unheard of. More likely, though, we will know
very early on that one of the alternatives is impossible. Then, we can
focus all our resources on developing the other.
Promises
When we first announced our intention to continue developing the eMail
client formerly known as QUALCOMM Eudora, we could not begin to
imagine the specific challenges ahead of us, but we had a good idea, in a
broad-brush and somewhat academic way, of The Things We Had To Do—
even before our mail client had a name. We understood:
that the way the "new Eudora" displayed rich-text messages had to
change;
that Eudora's interface was dependent on a toolkit that was not only
prohibitively expensive but also outdated;
that we'd need to support Eudora 7 until we'd released its successor;
and
that writing the "new Eudora" for Macintosh would be Hard Work.
We made certain commitments to our Windows backers and our users,
and we kept them—for the most part. Right now, we are using this space

Our Progress So Far
Although it's manifestly unsuitable for general release, HERMES Mail for
Windows now sends and receives messages. Another variant of HERMES
Mail does not have message-sending capability, but the interface has been
built from the ground up. The HERMES SSL Extensions are in wide use
and (when installed correctly) work correctly. On the other hand, some of
the details we published were wrong or impossible, and one might
summarise our work as "promises made—promises broken"... but that
would be wilful distortion of the truth. The big picture has been painted
true to the original description, and we simply need to fill in the details in a
different manner than originally intended.
We've been mostly silent about HERMES Mail/X (for reasons which will
soon become apparent). We now aim to correct this oversight, especially
given the number of people asking for their preferred platform to be
supported. Given that OS X is essentially a variant of UNIX, we've hit upon
a unique solution: as soon as HERMES Mail/Win is released, those who
need Mac OS X (and UNIX workstation) support Right The Freak Now will
merely need to install it and the Wine compatibility layer. That's not a
complete solution, though; we promised to offer a mail client for the Mac
(and UNIX workstation), not for simulated Windows (and all that implies).
Once again, though, there's no such thing as a free lunch. The previous
campaign raised sufficient money to create a quality product, but putting
that product into the hands of as many people as possible is a challenge
(largely due to media intransigence), and creating a whole new product
opens up a whole new can of worms, due to the many transitions that the
Apple architecture has undertaken since 2005 (certainly more than the PC,
which has remained largely stable since that date with the exception of the
x64 chipset).

Risks and challenges
The biggest hurdle, this time around, is manpower. The Mac has lower
market share than the PC, which is as true now as it was in 1997, but the
implications of that fact now and then are different. In the 1990s, the
Macintosh simply was not technically stable, meaning that its major users
were in education (and thus its "friendliness" was paramount).
Programmers for it, therefore, were harder to find. To-day, the Macintosh
has become entirely divorced from that heritage and instead is UNIX with
graphics tweaks. While programmers for UNIX have always been and still
are easy to find, they have always been and still are more expensive to
employ.
Beyond this lies the issue of the code itself. Because Eudora was written
on 1990's Macs, then ported to 2000's UNIX/Mac hybrids, and now stands
to be ported again to 2010's pure UNIX, there has been and will be far
more upheaval in the source than there was with the WIndows version.
The original code was written for CodeWarrior, a product no longer
supported by its manufacturer; Carbon is outdated and must be
transitioned to Cocoa; and so on, and so forth.
In general, we will need to employ more programmers this time around,
and they will come at a more expensive price. Still, we will create the
product—and finally, Apple users will no longer be subjected to the
tortures of Apple Mail.
